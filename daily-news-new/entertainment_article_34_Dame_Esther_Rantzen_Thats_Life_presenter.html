Dame Esther Rantzen, who has died at the age of 86, was best known for presenting the BBC TV programme That's Life!

Over two decades, what started as a consumer programme with quirky asides became renowned for its high-profile campaigns, notably against child abuse.

It resulted in Dame Esther setting up Childline, a national telephone helpline for children suffering abuse or neglect.

She later became a renowned campaigner for older people and, in the last years of her life, for the legalisation of medically-assisted death.

Esther Louise Rantzen was born in Berkhamsted, Hertfordshire on 22 June 1940. Her paternal ancestors were Polish Jews.

Son père, Henry, qui travaillait comme ingénieur de radiodiffusion pour la BBC, avait déménagé la famille de Londres pour échapper aux bombardements allemands pendant la Seconde Guerre mondiale.

Her father, Henry, who worked as a broadcast engineer for the BBC, had moved the family out of London to escape German bombing during World War Two.

The family returned to London when the war ended. She later recalled she hated the grey streets of the capital after the semi-rural idyll in which she had spent her early childhood.

Après un séjour à une école privée, dirigée par ce qu'elle se souvenait être une directrice sadique, elle est allée à l'École collégiale de Londres du Nord à Edgware, puis au Somerville College, à Oxford, où elle a lu l'anglais.

After a spell at a private school, run by what she recalled was a sadistic headmistress, she went to the North London Collegiate School in Edgware, and then to Somerville College, Oxford, where she read English.

Rantzen then secured a job as a trainee studio manager with BBC Radio, before moving to television as a researcher on the satirical show BBC3, which had been created by Ned Sherrin.

The relationship caused huge tensions in her department, not least because Wilcox's wife Patsy, one of Rantzen's best friends, also worked at the BBC.

By 1968, she was working on Braden's Week, a consumer programme fronted by Canadian presenter Bernard Braden.

A decision to allow the researchers a short spot on air gave Rantzen her first on-screen TV experience, where she made a favourable impression on the producers.

In 1972, Braden was sacked by the BBC after he appeared in a commercial for Stork margarine and his show was dropped.

A year later, the programme was resurrected, given a new title, That's Life! and Rantzen was selected as the main presenter.

Braden was sanguine about Rantzen's promotion but his wife, TV presenter Barbara Kelly, was publicly contemptuous of the decision and accused Wilcox of being instrumental in her appointment.

Launched in May 1973, That's Life! became an almost instant hit.

Recorded just before transmission on Saturday nights, it was initially a consumer protection programme investigating viewers' complaints about shoddy service and faulty goods.

Over the years it grew to include more quirky items.

Elle a lancé le prix Jobsworth pour les bureaucrates et les fonctionnaires du conseil trop zélés, et le "Heap of the Week", dans lequel les appareils ménagers défaillants seraient détruits à l'écran de diverses manières ingénieuses.

The show quickly became something of an institution but her relationship with Wilcox was causing problems behind the scenes.

Patsy Wilcox had reluctantly agreed to a divorce after Rantzen became pregnant, and Rantzen and Wilcox went on to marry in 1977.

But there were accusations from some BBC staff that Wilcox, who was her boss as well as her husband, was furthering her career, pointing out that BBC rules forbade married couples working together.

Wilcox resigned in 1980 and set up his own successful production company.

Rantzen also found time to devise a new talent series, The Big Time, which launched the career of singer Sheena Easton.

She became involved with the team behind the new ITV breakfast programme, TV-am, but dropped out before it went to air in 1983 so that she could remain at the BBC.

Dans les années 80, That's Life! avait élargi son mandat en éliminant les questions de base de la consommation pour prendre le rôle de militant.

By the 1980s, That's Life! had expanded its remit away from basic consumer issues to take on the role of campaigner.

L'apparition de Ben Hardwick, un enfant qui avait désespérément besoin d'une greffe de foie, a lancé une campagne visant à sensibiliser le public à la nécessité de donner des organes.

The appearance of Ben Hardwick, a child desperately in need of a liver transplant, launched a drive to raise the awareness of the need for organ donors. As a result, Ben received a new liver at the age of two, but died a year later.

D'autres campagnes ont permis de remplacer les surfaces dures des terrains de jeux pour enfants par des matériaux plus doux et de modifier la loi pour faire appliquer le port des ceintures de sécurité pour enfants à l'arrière des voitures.

Other campaigns helped introduce the replacement of hard surfaces in children's playgrounds with softer material and a change in the law to enforce the wearing of children's seat belts in the rear of cars.

Tout le monde n'était pas satisfait du nouveau format et il y avait des plaintes selon lesquelles une séquence sérieuse, parfois émotionnellement épuisante, était suivie d'un article bizarre sur, par exemple, des légumes à l'apparence rude.

Not everyone was happy with the new format and there were complaints that a serious, and sometimes emotionally draining sequence, was followed by a quirky item on, for example, rude-looking vegetables.

Le programme a continué à maintenir ses chiffres de visionnage élevés après un déménagement vers les soirées du dimanche, mais le paysage de la télévision a changé.

The programme continued to maintain its high viewing figures after a move to Sunday nights, but the television landscape was changing.

The BBC's show Watchdog and ITV's The Cook Report were bringing a much harder edge to consumer journalism.

The final That's Life! was broadcast on 19 June 1994 and featured a look back at both the highlights of the programme and Rantzen's own campaigning.

Le résultat le plus important et le plus durable de toute campagne That's Life! a été la création de Childline en 1986. Elle a suivi un certain nombre d'incidents, dont celui où un tout-petit avait été enfermé dans une chambre à coucher.

Possibly the most important and lasting result of any That's Life! campaign was the setting up of Childline in 1986. It followed a number of incidents, featured on the programme, including one in which a toddler had starved to death, locked in a bedroom.

Rantzen a convaincu la BBC après la diffusion du programme que la ligne d'aide devait être créée pour que les enfants souffrant d'abus ou de négligence puissent appeler en toute confiance.

Rantzen persuaded the BBC after the programme was transmitted that a helpline should be set up so that children suffering abuse or neglect could call in confidence. In the 48 hours it was open, it was inundated with calls.

Les professionnels de la garde d'enfants ont affirmé que ce serait impossible, mais elle a réussi à rassembler l'argent et à persuader BT de fournir des locaux et une ligne téléphonique gratuite.

This led to Rantzen campaigning for a 24/7 service to be set up. Childcare professionals claimed it would be impossible but she managed both to raise the money and persuade BT to provide premises and a free phone line.

En 2006, Childline a fusionné avec le NSPCC, avec 12 centres au Royaume-Uni et l'idée a été rapidement copiée dans de nombreux pays à l'étranger.

By the time Childline merged with the NSPCC in 2006, it had 12 centres in the UK and the idea was quickly copied in many countries abroad.

Rantzen also devised and presented Hearts of Gold, a programme that celebrated previously unknown people who had been responsible for good deeds.

When That's Life! came off air, Rantzen continued to present her own afternoon talk show, entitled Esther, on BBC Two. It ran for 600 episodes and received two Bafta nominations.

Desmond Wilcox est décédée en 2000 laissant Rantzen en deuil. Elle a écrit une série d'articles sur le problème de la solitude chez les personnes âgées, qu'elle décrit comme "un ennemi rampant qui érode la confiance".

Desmond Wilcox died in 2000 leaving Rantzen bereft. She wrote a series of articles about the problem of loneliness among older people, which she described as "a creeping enemy which erodes confidence."

Elle a finalement mis en place The Silver Line, un système de soutien téléphonique 24h/24 et 7j/7 qui offrait des conseils, des informations et du soutien à ceux qui se sentaient seuls et indésirables.

She eventually set up The Silver Line, a 24/7 phone support system which offered advice, information and support to those who felt alone and unwanted.

She wrote a number of regular columns for newspapers and magazines and also involved herself with more than 50 charities.

Following the MPs' expenses scandal in 2009, Rantzen announced she would stand for parliament in Luton South.

Moran stood down two days later but Rantzen fought the seat in the 2010 General Election, coming fourth with just over 4% of the vote.

Rantzen was made an OBE in 1991 and CBE in 2006. In the 2015 New Year Honours list, she was appointed a Dame Commander in recognition of the two helpline services she set up.

L'année suivante, elle a mis en garde contre la pression exercée par les médias sociaux sur les enfants dans le monde numérique, exhortant les familles à être " conscientes " de la question.

The following year she warned of the social media pressure on children in the digital world, urging families to "be aware" of the issue.

Speaking on Desert Island Discs in 2019, Dame Esther told presenter Lauren Laverne that her own broadcasting career would not be as successful if she had joined the industry now as a young woman, because she would not be "pretty enough".

A year later, she witnessed the House of Commons vote in favour of the first stage of a Bill that might make a medically-assisted death legal in England and Wales. A similar bill was introduced to the Scottish Parliament.

In September 2026, the House of Commons voted against the Assisted Dying Bill, a decision Dame Esther described as "a tragic day".
